## 1. Scope and questions

Stage 10 must let legal work be routed between: public research / redaction then send / approved private provider / local execution / non-disclosure. The evidence questions:

- Q1. What do E&W professional rules and guidance say about putting client or confidential information into AI tools, about provider due diligence, and about client consent?
- Q2. Legal professional privilege (E&W): when does it arise, how is it lost, and what is the position of (a) a solicitor on enterprise terms, (b) a founder with no lawyer, (c) a founder pasting a solicitor's advice into a consumer tool? US comparison and discoverability of chat logs.
- Q3. UK data protection: controller/processor roles with an AI provider, special category data, US transfers, DPIA, minimisation, anonymisation vs pseudonymisation, NCSC on LLM security.
- Q4. Trade secrets ("reasonable steps") and third-party NDA material.
- Q5. Security-sensitive material (credentials, vulnerabilities, architecture).
- Q6. Logs, retention, deletion; records of AI use vs minimising retained confidential data; disclosure exposure.
- Q7. Redaction and minimisation methods in actual use, and their documented limits.

## 3. Findings per question

### Q1. Professional duties (E&W; US comparator)

**1.1 Core rule.** SRA Code para 6.3 (as reported, I-01): "You keep the affairs of current and former clients confidential unless disclosure is required or permitted by law or the client consents." The Bar equivalent is Core Duty 6 and rC15.5 (I-05 para 28, text-verified). — *Supported finding* (rule); wording of 6.3 is fetch-summary.

**1.2 Entering client or confidential information into AI tools.** All four E&W bodies converge on a two-tier rule:

- Public / free / consumer tools: do not enter confidential information. Law Society (I-04, text-verified): "do not put any confidential data into the tool". Judiciary (I-06, text-verified): input "should be seen as being published to all the world". Bar Council (I-05 para 28, text-verified): "Be extremely vigilant"; sharing "is likely to be a breach of Core Duty 6 and rule rC15.5".
- Procured / closed tools: permitted in principle, conditionally. Law Society (I-04): for a product "contained solely within your firm's legal environment, you may wish to consider if and how" to input confidential data, "subject to the terms of use". SRA (I-03, as reported): only where "appropriate contractual, technical and organisational safeguards are in place"; and "Both free to use and paid for AI systems may pose risks".
- The Bar Council is stricter on personal data (I-05 para 29, text-verified): "You should never input any personal data in response to prompts from the system"; synthetic data is offered as "one possible way".

Standing: *supported finding*. Note the Bar Council and judiciary texts describe consumer-tool behaviour ("remember every question", I-06) more absolutely than NCSC does (I-30: a query is not automatically incorporated into the model, but is visible to and stored by the provider). The practical rule is the same; the technical premise differs.

**1.3 Due diligence on providers.** Law Society (I-04, text-verified): "carry out due diligence (including supplier due diligence) on the AI tools you use"; be able to say where data is processed, who processes it, how and where it is stored, who has access; plan an exit "including data removal and deletion". It also warns that tools "built on top of" platforms may share metadata (authorship, file names) with the underlying provider. Bar Council (I-05 paras 21, 22, 30): understand how the model uses inputs and "engage any relevant protective settings"; "Due diligence on the security protocols of AI tools is essential"; check terms against CD6 and rC15.5. CCBE (F-09) is consistent. — *Supported finding*.

**1.4 Client consent.** No inspected E&W source imposes an express rule that client consent is required before using an AI tool on client data.
- Law Society (I-04, text-verified): the SRA has no "specific guidance on generative AI related to use or disclosure of use for client care"; "It is advisable that you and your clients decide on whether and how generative AI tools might be used"; check "the client engagement agreement ... whether it can be input into a generative AI system".
- SRA confidentiality guidance (I-02, as reported) treats outsourcing as something terms of engagement "may set out", while warning clients "may not have agreed or understood".
- US contrast (I-39, secondary): ABA Formal Opinion 512 requires informed consent before inputting client information into self-learning tools, and "Boilerplate waivers will not suffice".
Standing: *context-dependent method* for E&W (consent/transparency is advised, not shown to be mandated); *supported finding* for the ABA position via a secondary source. Inference, not decided: under para 6.3 a disclosure to a provider that is neither consented to nor otherwise permitted is a breach, so engagement-letter coverage is the practical route.

**1.5 After an accidental disclosure.** I-07 para 21 (text-verified): a regulated professional who does this would need "to bring this to the attention of their regulator" and "be advised to consult with the Information Commissioner's Office". I-06: report as a data incident. — *Supported finding*.

**1.6 Relevance to `legal-skills` users.** These duties bind solicitors, barristers and in-house solicitors. Founders and engineers are not bound by them, but are bound by contract (NDAs), data protection law and their own interest in privilege (Q2–Q4). — *Inference*.

### Q2. Legal professional privilege

#### Decided law (E&W), as read on Find Case Law

- D1. LAP covers confidential lawyer–client communications; it is confined to members of the legal profession (F-27). *Three Rivers (No 5)* (I-08 paras 4, 31, text-verified): the client was the BIU; the Bank was "not entitled to privilege" in documents prepared by other employees. The Court of Appeal has twice said it is bound by this while doubting it (I-10 paras 134, 140; I-12 para 41).
- D2. LAP needs a dominant purpose of obtaining or giving legal advice (I-11 paras 100–102, partial read).
- D3. Litigation privilege test (I-10 para 64, text-verified, quoting Lord Carswell in *Three Rivers (No 6)* para 102): communications "between parties or their solicitors and third parties" for information or advice in connection with litigation, where litigation is in progress or contemplated, the communication is for the "sole or dominant purpose of conducting that litigation", and the litigation is "adversarial, not investigative or inquisitorial". *Three Rivers (No 6)* itself was not available on Find Case Law (404) and is read only through I-10 and I-12.
- D4. Limited waiver (I-09 paras 28–29, text-verified): privilege is not "waived generally because a privileged document has been disclosed for a limited purpose only"; scope depends on what was "expressly or impliedly communicated" and reasonably understood.
- D5. Tribunal observation on public AI tools (I-07 paras 21, 60, text-verified): uploading to "an open source AI tool, such as ChatGPT" is "to breach client confidentiality and waive legal privilege"; "Closed source AI tools which do not place information in the public domain, such as Microsoft Copilot, are available ... without these risks."

Weight of D5: it was not necessary to the decision (the case concerned fake citations and *Hamid* referrals); privilege was not argued; the technical description ("open source", "on the internet in the public domain") is loose; and an Upper Tribunal decision does not bind the High Court. I-16 says it "does not bind other courts" but is "likely to be at least persuasive". Treat it as strong persuasive guidance and as the regulator-adopted position (I-03), not as a fully reasoned holding.

#### Not decided anywhere inspected

- Whether disclosure to an AI provider under enterprise confidentiality terms preserves confidentiality for privilege purposes. D4 + D5's Copilot sentence point towards "yes"; no case decides it. I-18: "no direct English authority equivalent to Heppner". I-15 fn 2 and I-14 say the same for the US.
- Whether a non-lawyer's exchanges with an AI tool can attract litigation privilege.
- Whether a lawyer's prompts and outputs are protected as lawyers' working papers (asserted by I-17; no authority inspected).
- Cases named as research questions, not read: *Gotha City v Sotheby's* [1998] 1 WLR 114 (limited disclosure preserving confidentiality against the world); *B v Auckland District Law Society* [2003] UKPC 38; *Brown v Guardian Royal Exchange* [1994] 2 Lloyd's Rep 325; *Balabel v Air India* [1988] Ch 317. All but *Gotha City* are cited inside I-08/I-09.

| (a) Solicitor (or in-house solicitor) using an AI tool under enterprise terms | Duty of confidentiality applies (I-01). Privilege survives limited, confidential disclosure in general (D4). Enterprise terms fix confidentiality but not the absence of a lawyer (I-17). | *Supported finding* (no LAP); *unresolved question* (litigation privilege) |
| (c) Founder pastes solicitor's advice into a consumer tool | The advice was privileged when given. D5 treats upload to a public tool as loss of confidentiality and waiver. US: I-13 fn 3, text-verified — he "waived the privilege by sharing that information with Claude and Anthropic, just as if he had shared it with any other third party". | High risk that privilege in the pasted advice is lost; the new prompts/outputs are not themselves privileged (no lawyer). I-16: once waived, "no mechanism" to recover. Whether an English court would find full loss, where the provider's terms promise some confidentiality, is untested: D4 shows English law asks what was communicated and understood, which makes the provider's actual terms relevant. | *Supported finding* as a risk warning; extent of loss is an *unresolved question* |

Design consequence: the client owns the privilege, so a founder can destroy it without the lawyer knowing. Class 1 in §4 must be triggered by *what the material is* (a lawyer's advice), not by who the user is.

#### US comparison (as at 2026-09-19)

- *Heppner* (I-13, text-verified). Three grounds: (1) Claude "is not an attorney"; privileges need "a trusting human relationship"; (2) not confidential — "not merely because Heppner communicated with a third-party AI platform but also because" the consumer privacy policy allowed training and disclosure to "third parties", including "governmental regulatory authorities"; (3) not for legal advice — "perhaps presents a closer call". Dictum: "Had counsel directed Heppner to use Claude, Claude might arguably" have functioned like a lawyer's agent (*Kovel*). Sharing with counsel later does not "alchemically" create privilege. Work product failed: not prepared "at the behest of counsel".
- *Warner v Gilbarco* (ED Mich, 10 Feb 2026) — as reported by I-14/I-15, not inspected: a pro se litigant's ChatGPT materials were work product; no waiver because waiver of work product needs disclosure "to an adversary or in a way likely to get in an adversary's hand"; AI programs "are tools, not persons".
- The two are reconcilable: different doctrines (privilege waiver by any third-party disclosure; work-product waiver only by disclosure towards an adversary) and different facts (represented defendant acting alone vs litigant in person). I-14: "The law is unsettled and still developing."
- Counsel-crafted prompts are opinion work product: *Tremblay v OpenAI* (ND Cal 2024), *Concord Music v Anthropic* (ND Cal 2025) — as reported by I-15. A lawyer's "instruction" to log prompts does not by itself create privilege: *In re OpenAI* (SDNY 2025), as reported by I-15.
- Discoverability of provider-held logs: a 13 May 2025 order required OpenAI to preserve "all output log data that would otherwise be deleted"; lifted by order of 9 Oct 2025 with exceptions (I-19, weak secondary). *Heppner* itself cites *In re OpenAI* (5 Jan 2026) for the point that users lack "substantial privacy interests" in conversations with a public platform that the platform "retains in the normal course of its business" (I-13, text-verified). A search result (not inspected) reports a November 2025 order to produce 20 million de-identified chat logs.
- Standing: *supported finding* that user deletion settings do not guarantee deletion once the provider is under a litigation hold; *disputed/developing* on privilege and work product.

### Q3. Data protection (UK; EU note)

- 3.1 **No AI exemption; "personal data" is wider than "PII".** I-23 (text-verified): "There is no 'AI exemption' to data protection law"; compliance built around PII rests on "a fundamental misunderstanding". Incidental processing still counts. — *Supported finding*.
- 3.2 **Controller / processor.** A business that decides to put personal data into an AI tool for its own purposes is a controller for that processing. The provider's role is set by "the practical realities of the processing", not by the contract; for closed-access models the ICO thinks it "unlikely that such developers can claim to be processors at the deployment stage" and joint controllership "remains likely" (I-23, text-verified). Most enterprise terms nonetheless describe the provider as processor. — *Disputed claim* (regulator vs industry). Practical effect: a data processing agreement is necessary but does not settle the role; provider use of inputs for its own purposes (training, safety review) is the feature to look for.
- 3.3 **Special category and criminal-offence data.** Legal matters routinely contain health, union, belief, sex-life and offence data. I-24 lists processing of special category data among high-risk indicators; I-26: special category data sent under the data bridge "must correctly be identified by UK organisations as such when it is being shared". The Article 9 / Schedule 1 DPA 2018 condition analysis was not researched here. — *Supported finding* (heightened handling); conditions are an *unresolved question*.
- 3.4 **US transfers.** Mechanism: adequacy under SI 2023/1028 reg 3 for a recipient "indicated on the Data Privacy Framework List as participating in the UK Extension" (I-27, text-verified); otherwise Article 46 safeguards with a transfer risk assessment (I-25). Status 2026-09-19: legislation.gov.uk shows no revocation of SI 2023/1028 but carries only the as-made text; **not positively verified**. EU side: search results (not inspected) report the General Court upheld the EU–US DPF in *Latombe* (3 Sept 2025) and an appeal, C-703/25 P, is pending. The UK bridge is legally separate but depends on the same US certification scheme, so a CJEU invalidation would be a change-monitoring trigger. Checking the specific provider on the DPF List, including UK Extension participation, is a per-provider step. — *Context-dependent method*.
- 3.5 **DPIA.** Article 35(1) (quoted in I-24). ICO list: "Innovative technology ... (including AI). A DPIA is required where this processing is combined with any of the criteria from the European guidelines" (text-verified). Sensitive data plus AI will usually meet that. If no DPIA, "document your reasons". Guidance is under review following the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025. — *Supported finding*, dated.
- 3.6 **Minimisation and anonymisation.** I-20 (text-verified): "Pseudonymous information is still personal data"; anonymisation reduces "the amount of personal data you hold", pseudonymisation reduces "the risks". I-21: identifiability is "a spectrum"; test is "means reasonably likely to be used"; apply a "motivated intruder" test; address singling out and linkability ("mosaic or jigsaw effect"). Crucially (I-21, text-verified): the "whose hands" approach "only applies when disclosing information to an organisation who is not acting with you as a joint controller or as your processor" — for a processor, "the status of the data in your hands is what matters." So replacing names before sending a document to a provider that acts as your processor does **not** take the prompt outside UK GDPR while you hold the key or the original. It remains a valuable security and minimisation measure.
- 3.7 **EU divergence.** CJEU in *EDPS v SRB* (I-28, press release, text-verified): pseudonymised data is not personal data "in all cases and for every person"; it depends on whether the recipient can identify. That concerned Regulation 2018/1725 and a recipient, and the sender's own duties (eg transparency) still applied. It does not obviously displace the ICO's processor point. — *Unresolved question* for EU routing.
- 3.8 **NCSC.** I-30: a query "will be visible to the organisation providing the LLM"; "Those queries are stored"; sensitivity can come from "who is asking the question (and when)"; beware "aggregation of information across multiple queries"; stored queries "may be hacked, leaked, or more likely accidentally made publicly accessible"; the operator may be acquired. Advice: "not to include sensitive information in queries to public LLMs"; for sensitive use, a cloud-provided private LLM (check whether provider staff can view queries) or self-hosting. I-29: LLMs "simply do not enforce a security boundary between instructions and data"; prompt injection may "never be totally mitigated"; if residual risk is intolerable "it may not be a good use case for LLMs". — *Supported finding*. Consequence for agentic skills: an agent that holds confidential matter data *and* reads untrusted documents or web pages *and* can send data out is an exfiltration path; confidentiality routing must consider tool permissions, not only the model provider.

### Q4. Trade secrets and third-party NDA material

- 4.1 **Statutory test** (I-32, text-verified; UK-wide): information that is secret (not "generally known among, or readily accessible to, persons within the circles that normally deal with the kind of information"), "has commercial value because it is secret", and "has been subject to reasonable steps under the circumstances, by the person lawfully in control of the information, to keep it secret". — *Supported finding*.
- 4.2 **Effect of putting a secret into a third-party AI tool.** No UK decision found. I-34 (Oct 2025): "no judicial guidance in the United Kingdom on this requirement". Inference: a single disclosure to a provider bound by confidentiality terms does not make information "generally known"; but habitual pasting into tools whose terms allow training or provider reuse is evidence against "reasonable steps" and, if the content resurfaces, against secrecy. Conversely an AI-use policy, approved-tool list and no-training terms are evidence *for* reasonable steps (I-33, I-35: measures scale with value, from marking to encryption). — *Practical heuristic*; extent is an *unresolved question*. Note: I-33 overstates the law as "all reasonable steps"; the regulation says "reasonable steps under the circumstances".
- 4.3 **Third-party NDA material.** Not the user's secret to risk. Whether an AI provider is a permitted recipient depends on the NDA's wording (permitted recipients, purpose limit, sub-contractor and security clauses); this is contract interpretation per document, not a general rule. Breach can sound in contract and equitable confidence, and unlawful "use or disclosure" under the 2018 Regulations; I-35: an employer "can be liable" for an employee's infringement of a third party's trade secrets. The Bar Council expressly lists "trade secrets" among material not to share (I-05 para 28). No inspected source discusses AI tools and NDAs specifically. — *Practical heuristic*: default to non-disclosure or redaction unless the NDA permits service providers under equivalent confidentiality.
- 4.4 Separate IP point, flagged by I-17 only: disclosing an invention to a non-confidential tool may have consequences for "IP filings" (novelty). Not researched. — *Unresolved question*.

### Q5. Security-sensitive information

- No NCSC document inspected deals specifically with pasting credentials or vulnerability details into AI tools. The general rule in I-30 covers it: do not submit to public LLMs anything "that would lead to issues were they made public". OWASP LLM02 (I-31) lists "security credentials" as sensitive data and recommends detection and redaction by "pattern matching" before processing. I-06: use work devices and refuse app permissions.
- Inference: credentials and live secrets are the one class with **no legitimate reason** to be in a legal prompt at all — the legal question never turns on the value of an API key. Redaction loses nothing. Unpatched vulnerability details and security architecture are different: a breach-notification or contract-security analysis may genuinely need them, at an abstracted level; route to approved-private or local and minimise. — *Practical heuristic*.
- Prompt injection (I-29) adds a second risk: secrets reachable by an agent (environment variables, files) can be exfiltrated even if the user never pastes them.

### Q6. Logs, retention, deletion

- There is a real tension in the guidance:
  - Keep records: Law Society (I-04, text-verified): if the tool has no history, "it is advisable that you document all inputs, outputs and system errors". F-04/I-07 expect supervisors to be able to check AI-assisted work.
  - Minimise: judiciary (I-06): "disable the chat history"; "Even with history turned off ... it should be assumed that data entered is being disclosed". NCSC (I-30): stored queries are a breach target. UK GDPR storage limitation and minimisation apply to logs containing personal data (I-23: no exemption). Law Society exit planning: "data removal and deletion within the generative AI system".
- Disclosure exposure (E&W): a stored prompt or output is a "document" — "anything in which information of any description is recorded" (CPR 31.4, I-36, text-verified) — and documents held by a provider can be within a party's "control" if the party has "a right to inspect or take copies" (r 31.8). Privilege is the only shield, and per Q2 it will often be unavailable for non-lawyer use. US: see *Heppner* (seized from devices) and the OpenAI preservation order (provider-side retention beyond the user's settings).
- Resolution proposed (inference): separate the **audit record** from the **content**. Keep who / when / which tool / which matter / sensitivity class / what was checked; keep confidential content only in the matter file under its normal retention and privilege regime, not duplicated in tool histories. Prefer zero-retention or short-retention provider settings for classes 1–4. Do not describe any log as "privileged" by label alone (*In re OpenAI*, as reported, I-15). Never delete once litigation is reasonably contemplated (preservation duty — not researched here; flag for lawyer). — *Context-dependent method*.

### Q7. Redaction and minimisation methods, and their limits

Methods documented in inspected sources:

| Method | Source | Documented limit |
|---|---|---|
| Synthetic / fictional facts | I-05 para 29 ("synthetic data"); I-04 ("Always create and use fictional data" for testing and templates) | Offered for testing and prompting generally; a synthetic scenario that tracks a unique real deal can still be recognised (inference from I-21 singling out). |
| Masking / suppression of direct identifiers (names, companies, addresses, dates of birth) | I-21 free-text example | I-21: "You should not focus only on removing obvious information"; indirect identifiers and context remain. |
| Generalisation ("a place" for the place name; imprecise dates) | I-21 | Loses detail. In legal work dates, amounts, places and roles are often legally material (limitation, thresholds, jurisdiction, territorial scope) — inference; no inspected source measures this for legal tasks. |
| Consistent pseudonyms / tokenisation with a key held locally | I-22; I-31 ("tokenization ... pattern matching") | Still personal data (I-20), and for a processor "the status of the data in your hands is what matters" (I-21). Linkability: "mosaic or jigsaw effect". |
| Abstracting the legal question (ask the general point of law; keep facts local) | Implied by I-04 ("whether an alternative, more appropriate means can be used") and I-30 (queries that would not matter if public); no source describes it as a named method | The answer may not fit the real facts; applicability analysis must then be done locally. The question alone can be revealing: I-30's "CEO ... 'how best to lay off an employee?'" example. |
| Provider-side controls (history off, no-training opt-out) | I-06, I-05 para 21 | I-06: still "assumed that data entered is being disclosed"; preservation orders can override deletion (I-19). |

Cross-cutting limits:
- Free text is the hard case. I-21 (text-verified): anonymising qualitative material "does not lend itself to bulk processing and can require careful human judgement".
- Category-based de-identification (NER-style) is weaker than anonymisation; the benchmark built on ECtHR judgments exists because the gap is measurable (I-38, abstract).
- LLMs themselves re-identify: I-37 (abstract) reports attribute inference at up to 85% top-1 accuracy and that "text anonymization and model alignment, are currently ineffective" against it. One study, 2023, Reddit data — not legal documents; treat as a caution, not a measured rate for contracts.
- Redaction addresses data protection and some confidentiality risk. It does **not** by itself cure privilege risk: privilege attaches to the communication, and a pseudonymised copy of a solicitor's advice is still that advice. Whether disclosure of a de-identified version waives privilege is undecided. — *Unresolved question*.
- In small markets, deal facts (sector, size, timing, counterparty type) identify companies even with names removed. Company data is not personal data, but it is exactly what NDAs and trade-secret law protect (inference from I-21 linkability reasoning applied to commercial confidentiality).

Standing for Q7 overall: *context-dependent method*. No inspected professional source endorses redaction as sufficient on its own for client-confidential material; the professional bodies' primary control is tool choice (Q1.2), with redaction as a supplementary measure.

## 4. Proposed sensitivity classification

Routes: **P** public/consumer tool or public research; **R** redact/abstract then send; **A** approved private provider (enterprise terms: no training on inputs, confidentiality, access limits, deletion, DPA, transfer mechanism checked); **L** local execution; **N** non-disclosure (do not process with any AI; human/lawyer only). "Minimum handling" is the least protective route that the evidence supports; a stricter route is always allowed. Classes are cumulative: material takes the strictest class that applies.

| # | Class | Examples | Minimum handling | Justification |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Public | Legislation, published judgments, regulator guidance, the abstract legal question with no matter context | P. But consider whether the *question itself* reveals something (who is asking, when). | I-06 (only information "already in the public domain"); I-30 (sensitivity from who asks; aggregation) |
| 1 | Privileged material | A solicitor's or barrister's advice; instructions to lawyers; drafts exchanged with lawyers; litigation preparation once a dispute is contemplated | Never P. A or L only, and for a non-lawyer user only after a warning that the holder risks waiver and that new AI exchanges are not themselves privileged; recommend asking the lawyer first. Redaction does not cure privilege risk. Restrict who can read stored outputs. | I-07 paras 21, 60; I-13 fn 3; I-09 (limited waiver depends on terms); F-27; I-16; I-05 para 28 |
| 2 | Client-confidential (professional duty) — user is a regulated lawyer handling client affairs | Client instructions, client documents, matter facts | Never P. A or L, with supplier due diligence done, engagement terms or client agreement covering the tool, and minimisation. Incident → regulator and ICO consideration. | I-01 para 6.3; I-03; I-04; I-05 paras 28–30; I-02; I-07 para 21; I-39 (US consent comparator) |
| 3 | Special category or criminal-offence personal data | Health details in an employment dispute; allegations of offences; union membership; immigration decision letters | Never P. R where the identifiers are not legally needed, otherwise A or L; DPIA screening; transfer mechanism verified per provider; identify as sensitive under the data bridge. Pseudonymisation does not remove UK GDPR. | I-24; I-26; I-27; I-20; I-21; I-23; I-05 para 29 |
| 4 | Ordinary personal data | Names, emails, job titles of employees, customers, counterparties' staff | Not P (consumer terms). R by default — most legal questions do not need real names; else A or L with DPA and transfer check. | I-23; I-21; I-25; I-04; I-05 para 29 |
| 5 | Own confidential commercial information and trade secrets | Source code, pricing, roadmap, unreleased product design, cap table, fundraising terms | Not P where terms allow training or provider reuse. A or L; or R/abstraction. Record the approved-tool policy as a "reasonable step". | I-32; I-34; I-35; I-33 (weak); I-30 |
| 6 | Third-party confidential information under NDA or contract | Counterparty data-room documents; supplier pricing; a customer's security questionnaire answers | Default N or R until the NDA's permitted-recipient and purpose clauses are checked; then A or L only if permitted. Never P. | I-32 (unlawful use/disclosure); I-35 (employer liability); I-05 para 28 (trade secrets); I-04 ("agreements between you and your client") — general rule is inference |
| 7 | Security-sensitive — live secrets | Credentials, API keys, private keys, tokens, connection strings | N always: strip before any route, including A and L logs. No legal task needs the value. Pattern-match and block. | I-31; I-30; I-29 (agent exfiltration); I-06 (security) |
| 8 | Security-sensitive — descriptive | Unpatched vulnerability details, incident forensics, security architecture, pen-test reports | Never P. L preferred; A if the provider's staff-access terms are known; abstract to the level the legal question needs (eg "unauthenticated access to customer records for N days"). | I-30 (can provider staff view queries?); I-29; I-31 |
| 9 | Regulatory, investigation or dispute-sensitive non-privileged material | Internal notes on a suspected breach; a non-lawyer's own analysis of legal exposure; draft admissions | Warn that AI exchanges are disclosable documents and are not privileged without a lawyer; prefer involving a lawyer first (Zone B in Stage 1 terms). If proceeding: A or L, minimal retention. | I-36 (CPR 31.4, 31.8); F-27; I-13; I-17; I-08 (internal documents outside LAP) |

Notes:
- "Approved private provider" is not a fixed list. The evidence supports a checklist, not a brand: no training on inputs; provider staff access limits; retention and deletion; sub-processors and underlying platforms (I-04 metadata point); location and transfer mechanism (I-25, I-27); incident notification; what happens under legal process (I-13's reliance on the privacy policy's disclosure clause shows courts read these terms). I-07's naming of one product is an example in a judgment, not an approval.
- Class 1 and class 9 are the two where the *user's status* (lawyer or not) changes the legal outcome; the skill should ask, not assume.
- The classification deliberately does not rely on redaction to downgrade class 1, 6 or 7.

### Unresolved questions
