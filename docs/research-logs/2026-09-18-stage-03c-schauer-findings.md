### 2. Material findings

#### SC1-01
- **ID:** SC1-01
- **Location:** ch 1 (unnumbered), pp. 7-10; restated ch 2 §2.4, p. 35
- **Problem addressed:** What makes legal reasoning different from ordinary good judgment, and therefore what a legal-production system must not collapse into.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** The characteristic legal methods (rule-following, precedent, deference to authority) all have one odd property: each can require an outcome other than the one that would be best for the matter in hand, all things considered. Law aims at getting the category of cases right, accepting some wrong results in individual cases for the sake of predictability, uniform treatment and limits on individual discretion. A rule that gives the fair answer here may give bad answers across the class.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** Descriptive claim about common-law systems, chiefly the US; the author says these methods are concentrated in law, not unique to it, and not the only thing lawyers do. He explicitly declines to argue that this is always desirable (pp. 11-12).
- **Production responsibility affected:** reasoning/applicability, risk communication, evaluation
- **Workflow / decision implication:** Keep two questions separate in every analysis: (a) what the governing rule, precedent or authority indicates, and (b) what outcome seems fair, sensible or commercially reasonable. Never let (b) silently replace (a). When they diverge, report the divergence as such. When proposing a rule, clause or position, test it against other foreseeable cases in the class, not just the present facts.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** Given a scenario where the sympathetic outcome conflicts with a clear rule, the output states the rule-based result first and labels the equitable argument separately. Fail if the output reasons from fairness to a conclusion and presents it as the legal position.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** A general-purpose model's pull toward the "reasonable" answer; equally, the opposite misuse of treating every rule as mechanically decisive (see SC1-07, SC1-08).
- **Repair implication:** Add an explicit "rule-indicated result vs all-things-considered result" split to applicability templates; reviewer check for conclusions justified only by fairness language.
- **Proposed disposition:** retain — foundational framing for applicability analysis.

#### SC1-02
- **ID:** SC1-02
- **Location:** ch 1, pp. 3-6 and p. 11
- **Problem addressed:** How far doctrinal reasoning actually determines outcomes, and therefore how much predictive confidence doctrinal analysis can support.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** Sceptics (Legal Realists, political scientists studying apex courts, psychologists, Bentham) hold that ideology, policy preference and ordinary human cognition explain outcomes more than doctrine does, and that decision-makers may sincerely misdescribe their own reasoning. The author treats the existence of distinctively legal constraint as an empirical claim: pointing to instances of genuine constraint says almost nothing about how often it happens. He also accepts that law is not a closed system; not every move in legal argument is found in legal rules, and the unexpected forces law outside existing rules.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** The author's working premise is that legal reasoning is real and widespread, but he concedes the sceptical position "may in the final analysis be sound" (p. 11). Fuller treatment is deferred to ch 7 (not in this range).
- **Production responsibility affected:** risk communication, evaluation, research
- **Workflow / decision implication:** Doctrinal analysis supports a statement of what the authorities indicate; it does not by itself support a probability of outcome, particularly in apex-court or politically charged matters. Confidence language should distinguish "the authorities indicate X" from "a tribunal will decide X".
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** Outputs that predict outcomes name the basis for prediction beyond doctrine (forum, level of court, contested-ness) or decline to predict. Fail if doctrinal clarity is converted directly into outcome certainty for a contested appellate question.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Using the sceptical view as licence to ignore doctrine; or ignoring it and over-promising.
- **Repair implication:** Separate fields for "legal position" and "outcome risk" in advice outputs.
- **Proposed disposition:** qualify — contrary evidence to any claim that doctrinal reasoning alone yields reliable prediction; needs ch 7 material to complete.

#### SC1-03
- **ID:** SC1-03
- **Location:** ch 2 §2.1, pp. 13-18
- **Problem addressed:** What it means for a rule to "apply": is the operative thing the words or the goal behind them?
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** Every rule has a background justification, but the rule is a concrete, more specific instantiation of it, and it is the instantiation that ordinarily carries legal force. Justifications are reduced to specific rules precisely because bare goals ("be reasonable") give poor guidance, invite manipulation and produce inconsistent enforcement. The wording is therefore important in itself, not a transparent window onto purpose: it is nearly always the starting point even where it is not the end point. Conduct within the purpose but outside the words is not caught by that rule; conduct within the words but outside the purpose ordinarily is.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** Applies to statutes, regulations, procedural rules and, by extension, contractual terms and internal policies written as rules. The author flags that interpretation proper is treated in ch 8 (not read). In a footnote he also notes that official enforcement practice can diverge from the written rule (ch 2 n. 2), deferred to ch 7.
- **Production responsibility affected:** reasoning/applicability, issue spotting, drafting, review/redlining
- **Workflow / decision implication:** Begin applicability analysis from the operative text and its defined terms. Record the rule's purpose as a separate item. Do not conclude "not caught" because the mischief is absent, or "caught" because the mischief is present, without a textual route.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** For a rule with a bright-line trigger, the output applies the trigger as written and identifies purpose only as a secondary argument. Fail if the analysis substitutes the purpose for the trigger.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Treating this as a universal literalism rule; the author says text is first word, not always last (see SC1-06, SC1-07).
- **Repair implication:** Applicability template with ordered fields: operative words, defined terms, literal result, purpose, whether purpose and literal result align.
- **Proposed disposition:** retain — directly operational.

#### SC1-04
- **ID:** SC1-04
- **Location:** ch 2 §2.2, pp. 18-20
- **Problem addressed:** Distinguishing clear applications of a rule from debatable ones.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** A rule has a settled centre, where ordinary meaning of its terms resolves the question, and a debatable edge, where it does not. At the edge, resort to the rule's purpose may be needed, and different candidate purposes produce different answers for the same item (a safety purpose and a noise purpose sort the borderline items differently). The author records in a footnote that Fuller disputed whether plain meaning can ever settle a case without purpose (n. 12).
- **Assumptions and applicability:** General to rule application; the core/fringe boundary is itself a judgment. The author's own real-case illustration was one where competent arguments existed both ways.
- **Production responsibility affected:** reasoning/applicability, issue spotting, risk communication
- **Workflow / decision implication:** Classify each applicability question as core or fringe and say so. For fringe questions, identify the candidate purposes, show how each would resolve the item, and cite the source for the purpose. Reserve confident language for core applications.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** On a mixed set of clear and borderline items under one rule, the output answers the clear ones plainly and flags only the borderline ones, giving purpose-dependent reasoning for those. Fail if all items get the same hedging or the same confidence.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Assuming a single obvious purpose; inventing a purpose without a source; treating a fringe case as core (see SC1-07).
- **Repair implication:** Add a core/fringe tag and a "purpose source" field to each applicability conclusion.
- **Proposed disposition:** retain — gives a usable two-tier classification.

#### SC1-05
- **ID:** SC1-05
- **Location:** ch 2 §2.2, pp. 20-23 (and ch 2 nn. 1, 17, 18); echoed ch 5 §5.1, pp. 89-90
- **Problem addressed:** Distorted impressions of legal uncertainty caused by learning law from reported cases.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** Disputes reach court, and especially appellate courts, mainly when both sides think they can win, which usually means the question sits at the edge of a rule. Reported decisions and teaching materials are therefore a biased sample: the routine, uncontested operation of rules is invisible in them. Inferring from case law that rules rarely give clear answers is a serious mistake; most legal events are easy cases. A footnote adds a qualification: parties sometimes litigate weak positions strategically, so the filter is imperfect. The author also says genuine constraint by precedent is least visible at the apex court and far more common in intermediate appellate courts.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** US-centred statistics, but the mechanism is general. Particularly relevant to any system whose knowledge of law is derived from corpora dominated by appellate opinions.
- **Production responsibility affected:** risk communication, research, evaluation, scoping
- **Workflow / decision implication:** Do not treat the existence of litigation on a provision as evidence that its application to the present facts is uncertain; check whether the present facts sit in the litigated edge or the unlitigated centre. Do not manufacture two-sided arguments for core applications. Conversely, absence of case law on a clear application is expected, not a gap.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** Evaluation suites include easy cases with a single right answer; pass requires a direct answer without invented doubt. Separately, outputs must not cite "there is much litigation on this section" as the sole reason for an uncertainty rating.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** A model trained on appellate opinions over-hedging everything; or reading the point as "most questions are easy" and under-flagging real fringe questions.
- **Repair implication:** Calibrate uncertainty ratings to the facts' position relative to the rule, not to the volume of case law; add easy-case regression tests.
- **Proposed disposition:** retain — important calibration principle for an AI system and for benchmark design.

#### SC1-06
- **ID:** SC1-06
- **Location:** ch 2 §2.3, pp. 24-29
- **Problem addressed:** A second kind of hard case: the words clearly apply (or clearly do not) but that result does not serve the rule's purpose.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** Because rules are generalizations addressed to many people over time, they are inevitably over-inclusive (catching cases the purpose would not) and under-inclusive (missing cases the purpose would catch). This is structural, not a drafting fault: drafters cannot foresee everything, and a rule detailed enough to be always right would be too complex to guide. Such cases differ from fringe cases: the language is not unclear; the dispute is whether the clear language should yield. The author gives examples going both ways, including fixed numerical thresholds that have drifted far from their original purpose yet still govern by their words, and cases where courts declined to apply clear words to situations remote from the legislative aim.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** General to rule-based instruments including contracts and policies. Examples are US; outcomes in the examples are not current authority.
- **Production responsibility affected:** reasoning/applicability, issue spotting, drafting, risk communication, change impact
- **Workflow / decision implication:** Tag hard applicability questions by type: (A) linguistic indeterminacy at the edge, or (B) clear text diverging from purpose. For type B, report the literal result as the baseline, then the purposive counter-argument, then which way the relevant forum tends to go. In drafting and review, test a proposed rule for both over- and under-inclusion against realistic scenarios and decide consciously which error to tolerate; note where numeric thresholds or technology-specific terms will age.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** Given a clear-text/purpose-mismatch scenario, the output identifies it as such (not as "ambiguous") and states both results. For drafting review, the output lists at least one over-inclusion and one under-inclusion scenario for a bright-line clause.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Calling type-B cases "ambiguous"; assuming purpose prevails; drafting ever-longer rules to eliminate all mismatch.
- **Repair implication:** Add hard-case type to the applicability schema; add an over/under-inclusion stress test to drafting review.
- **Proposed disposition:** retain — sharp, reusable distinction.

#### SC1-07
- **ID:** SC1-07
- **Location:** ch 2 §2.4, pp. 29-30
- **Problem addressed:** The legitimate and illegitimate senses of "formalism", and a characteristic analytical vice.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** One thing called formalism is a fault: presenting a contestable choice as if the plain meaning of a word compelled it, that is, acting as though one were at the core of a rule when one is really at the fringe. The author regards this as deception or self-deception. A different thing also called formalism is simply following clear words despite a poor result in the instance; that, he argues, is central to law and often defensible.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** General. The criticised vice is about candour in reasoning, so it applies to any written legal analysis.
- **Production responsibility affected:** reasoning/applicability, risk communication, review/redlining, evaluation
- **Workflow / decision implication:** Where a conclusion depends on choosing among available meanings or purposes, say that a choice is being made and on what basis. Do not use "plainly", "clearly" or "necessarily" for fringe conclusions.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** Reviewer check: for each conclusion expressed with certainty language, is the term's application genuinely core? Fail if an open-textured term ("reasonable", "material", "liberty"-type words) is applied with certainty language and no acknowledgement of alternatives.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Fluent AI prose makes disguised choice especially likely; also over-correction into hedging core cases (SC1-05).
- **Repair implication:** Lint for certainty adverbs attached to fringe-tagged conclusions.
- **Proposed disposition:** retain — maps directly to a checkable failure mode.

#### SC1-08
- **ID:** SC1-08
- **Location:** ch 2 §2.4, pp. 29-35 (incl. n. 30)
- **Problem addressed:** When text and purpose conflict, which prevails, and who is entitled to decide that.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** There is no uniform answer. In the US both letter-based and purpose-based arguments are professionally respectable and neither dominates; the author notes other systems, the UK among them, are somewhat less willing to depart from words. He reframes the debate: the real question is not which result is best in the case but whether this type of decision-maker should be empowered to set clear words aside. He adds two asymmetries: (i) words yielding to the rule's own purpose is fairly common, whereas a rule's purpose yielding to general justice is much rarer and mostly confined to extreme cases; (ii) courts frequently enforce rules they regard as bad on the ground that change belongs to the legislature. Procedural requirements, formalities and deadlines are his recurring examples of words being enforced despite hardship.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** US-centred; comparative remark on the UK is a single footnoted generalization and needs jurisdiction-specific research. The author declares his own sympathy for the text-respecting side (n. 38 and Preface), so his framing is not neutral.
- **Production responsibility affected:** reasoning/applicability, risk communication, escalation, drafting, review/redlining
- **Workflow / decision implication:** (1) On a text/purpose conflict, present both lines, state the jurisdiction's tendency with a source, and do not assure the purposive result. (2) Treat compliance-type requirements (time limits, filing formalities, execution formalities, exact descriptions) literally in planning and review; never advise reliance on a purposive rescue. (3) A decision to proceed on a reading that departs from clear words is not one for the agent: escalate to the responsible lawyer. (4) Arguments from general fairness against both text and purpose should be marked as low-probability except on extreme facts.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** Deadline scenario with "before [date]" wording and an action on that date: pass requires the literal-risk warning and no assurance. Text/purpose conflict memo: pass requires both arguments and a sourced statement of forum tendency.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Importing US purposivism or US textualism into another jurisdiction; agent deciding by itself that "the drafter obviously meant".
- **Repair implication:** Escalation trigger "departure from clear operative words"; jurisdiction field for interpretive approach in the authority/knowledge record.
- **Proposed disposition:** adapt — the who-decides framing converts well into an escalation rule; interpretive tendencies need jurisdiction-specific research.

#### SC1-09
- **ID:** SC1-09
- **Location:** ch 3 §3.1, pp. 36-37; §3.6, pp. 57-60; ch 4 §4.3, pp. 75-76
- **Problem addressed:** The two different relationships a prior decision can have to the deciding court, and how strong each is.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** Vertical precedent runs from higher to lower courts in the same hierarchy; the lower court may not overrule or disregard it. Horizontal precedent (stare decisis) is a court's obligation to its own earlier decisions; it genuinely constrains but can be overcome. Even mandatory authority is not absolute in a conflict: an obligation to an intermediate court is displaced by a conflicting decision of a higher one. The author prefers to describe obligations as real but outweighable rather than all-or-nothing. A footnote reminds that "the law laid down" by a leading case includes later decisions on the subject (ch 3 n. 40).
- **Assumptions and applicability:** Common-law hierarchies. US federal/state illustrations; strength of stare decisis varies by jurisdiction (the author says it is stronger in England, ch 3 n. 45) and is historically recent.
- **Production responsibility affected:** authority verification, research, reasoning/applicability, risk communication
- **Workflow / decision implication:** An authority record must capture the deciding court, its position relative to the forum that would hear the matter, and therefore whether the decision is vertically binding, horizontally binding, or neither for that forum. The same case has different status in different forums, so status is a relation, not a property of the case. Always check later decisions of the same or higher courts before stating what a leading case establishes.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** Given the same decision and two different forums, the output assigns different status correctly. Fail if a record stores "binding: yes/no" without a forum.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Flat "binding/persuasive" flags; treating an apex court's own precedent as immovable, or as freely revisable.
- **Repair implication:** Make forum a required key in authority-status computation; add subsequent-history check as a gate.
- **Proposed disposition:** retain — directly shapes the authority record.

#### SC1-10
- **ID:** SC1-10
- **Location:** ch 3 §3.2-3.3, pp. 37-44; ch 4 §4.1, pp. 61-67
- **Problem addressed:** Why a source counts in legal argument: because it is right, or because of what it is.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** Following precedent is distinct from learning from an earlier decision. If a court adopts a result because it is convinced by the reasoning, the earlier case is functioning as a teacher, not as precedent; precedent bites only when the court would otherwise decide differently. Rules and precedents are authoritative in a content-independent way: force comes from source and status, not from soundness, and a binding authority displaces the decision-maker's own assessment of the merits. Things labelled "authorities" are often used non-authoritatively, as stores of wisdom or information. The author records that the rationality of deferring to authority is itself long contested, and he gives the standard justifications for stare decisis (reliance, settlement, decisional economy) alongside its famous critics.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** Conceptual; general to common-law reasoning.
- **Production responsibility affected:** authority verification, reasoning/applicability, research, risk communication
- **Workflow / decision implication:** Record, for each source used, two independent attributes: status for this forum (what it is) and quality/fit of reasoning (what it says). Status governs whether it must be dealt with; quality governs how persuasive it is where status does not compel. A binding authority with weak reasoning is still binding; a well-reasoned non-binding source is still non-binding. State in the output which role each cited source is playing.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** Scenario with an adverse binding decision the model would regard as poorly reasoned and a favourable, well-reasoned out-of-jurisdiction decision: pass requires the adverse decision to be treated as controlling unless distinguishable. Fail if reasoning quality is used to demote binding authority.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Language models naturally weigh by persuasiveness of content; this is the wrong default for mandatory sources.
- **Repair implication:** Separate "status" and "persuasive weight" columns; rule that status is computed before content is assessed.
- **Proposed disposition:** retain — central to authority handling.

#### SC1-11
- **ID:** SC1-11
- **Location:** ch 3 §3.4, pp. 44-52 (incl. n. 19)
- **Problem addressed:** Deciding whether an earlier case is a precedent for the present one, and at what level of generality it speaks.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** No two cases are identical, and any two are alike in some respects and different in others, so similarity cannot be read off the facts. There are no natural legal similarities: whether two things are alike depends on the legal purpose in play (the same two items can be alike for one body of law and unalike for another), though law sometimes does adopt everyday categories. Taking the reported facts plus the outcome does not tell you what the case stands for, because the facts can be described at many levels of abstraction. Saying that only "material" facts count does not help: if materiality is fixed by some other rule, that rule, not the case, is doing the work; if it is not fixed, the later court can stress likenesses or differences as it pleases and constraint looks illusory. Near-identical ("on all fours") cases are the easy exception.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** Common-law case analysis. The author's sceptical argument is an intermediate step; his partial answer is SC1-12.
- **Production responsibility affected:** research, authority verification, reasoning/applicability, risk communication
- **Workflow / decision implication:** Never assert that a case "applies" on the strength of factual resemblance alone. State the proposition the case is said to stand for, the level of generality chosen, and where that level comes from. Where the present facts differ materially from the precedent's, present the narrower and broader readings and say the choice is open.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** Given a precedent summary containing only facts and outcome, the output offers multiple candidate readings at different generality and flags that the case alone does not settle which. Fail if it picks one reading without acknowledging alternatives.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Embedding-style "similar facts" retrieval presented as legal relevance; over-reading a case to its broadest formulation because that helps the client.
- **Repair implication:** Authority record field "proposition relied on" with "generality source: court's words / later cases / our inference".
- **Proposed disposition:** retain — also a direct caution for similarity-based retrieval.

#### SC1-12
- **ID:** SC1-12
- **Location:** ch 3 §3.4, pp. 52-54 (incl. n. 30)
- **Problem addressed:** A workable way to fix what a precedent stands for.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** Usually the precedent court says why it decided as it did, and in doing so describes the parties and events as instances of categories. Those stated categories and reasons are the primary guide to what the case stands for; where the court announces a rule in general terms the question largely disappears. Where the court used narrower categories, later parties can argue for extension or restriction but neither can claim the case compels their reading. In systems where each appellate judge gives a separate opinion, only reasoning shared by a majority of the judges counts, which makes extraction harder; the author notes fragmented apex-court decisions raise the same difficulty.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** Works best for single-majority-opinion systems (US). Expressly harder in the UK and some Commonwealth courts. The author's reliance on the court's stated reasons is in tension with the traditional holding/dicta line (SC1-13), which he acknowledges.
- **Production responsibility affected:** authority verification, research, reasoning/applicability
- **Workflow / decision implication:** Extract the court's own formulation of its rule and its own categorisation of the facts, with pinpoint cites, and distinguish these from headnotes or the agent's paraphrase. For multi-opinion decisions, tabulate each judge's reasons and rely only on the overlap commanding a majority; flag where no common reasoning exists.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** Output quotes or pinpoints the court's own rule statement for each proposition relied on; for a three-judgment decision with divergent reasons, identifies only the shared ground as the ratio. Fail if a single judge's reasoning in a split decision is reported as the holding.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Relying on editorial summaries; treating a concurring or dissenting opinion as the court's position.
- **Repair implication:** Pinpoint requirement in citation schema; "opinion type" and "majority support" fields.
- **Proposed disposition:** retain — concrete extraction method; qualify by jurisdiction.

#### SC1-13
- **ID:** SC1-13
- **Location:** ch 3 §3.5, pp. 54-57; §3.6, pp. 57-58
- **Problem addressed:** Distinguishing holding from dicta, and how much that distinction is worth in practice.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** Conventionally the holding is the legal rule that, applied to the facts, produces the result (it may be taken to include the stated reasons and the application), and everything not needed for the result is dicta: remarks on issues not before the court, surveys of an area, asides. The author doubts the line's firmness: reasons are always wider than the result they support, so the very statements that let a case be generalised are, strictly, unnecessary to the outcome. The distinction is clear only at the extremes. He adds a practical observation: before a lower court, arguing that adverse language from a higher court is "mere dicta" is a steep climb and usually fails, whereas distinguishing the case on its facts is the realistic route.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** US-centred practice observation; ch 9 (not read) returns to the topic. Other jurisdictions may police the ratio/obiter line more strictly; needs research.
- **Production responsibility affected:** authority verification, reasoning/applicability, risk communication
- **Workflow / decision implication:** Tag each relied-on passage as holding, reasoning supporting the holding, or peripheral remark, with a confidence level. Do not discount adverse higher-court language solely because it can be labelled dicta; present that as a weak argument. Do not present a favourable peripheral remark as what the court "held".
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** Given an adverse statement unnecessary to the result in a higher-court opinion, pass requires a warning that the statement is likely to be followed and that a dicta argument is weak. Given a favourable aside, pass requires it not be described as a holding.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Binary holding/dicta flags applied with false precision; "the court held" used for any sentence in an opinion.
- **Repair implication:** Three-way passage tag plus verb discipline ("held" / "reasoned" / "observed").
- **Proposed disposition:** qualify — useful, but the author himself marks the distinction as unstable.

#### SC1-14
- **ID:** SC1-14
- **Location:** ch 3 §3.6, pp. 58-59; ch 4 §4.2, p. 68
- **Problem addressed:** What a lower court (and so an advocate or adviser) may do with a binding precedent.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** "Binding" means follow or distinguish. A court may not concede that a binding case applies and then refuse to apply it, nor overrule it; but it may hold that the present facts differ in a relevant way, and a great deal of legal argument consists of one side asserting control and the other asserting a distinction. Successful distinctions in the author's examples worked by identifying a limit in the precedent's rationale that the present facts fell outside.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** Common-law litigation and advice on litigation risk; also relevant to opinion work where an adverse authority exists.
- **Production responsibility affected:** reasoning/applicability, research, risk communication, review/redlining
- **Workflow / decision implication:** For every adverse binding authority, the analysis must either accept its result or set out a specific distinction tied to the precedent's stated rationale and assess its strength. For every favourable binding authority, anticipate the distinctions the other side will draw. Ignoring an on-point adverse authority is never an option.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** Output addresses each on-point adverse authority supplied in the record with "follow" or a reasoned "distinguish". Fail if an adverse binding case in the record is omitted or dismissed as wrong.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Distinctions based on legally irrelevant factual differences; treating "distinguishable" as a label rather than an argument.
- **Repair implication:** Adverse-authority table with columns: rationale, proposed distinction, link of distinction to rationale, strength.
- **Proposed disposition:** retain.

#### SC1-15
- **ID:** SC1-15
- **Location:** ch 3 §3.6, pp. 59-60; ch 4 §4.3, p. 76; ch 3 §3.3, pp. 42-44
- **Problem addressed:** Assessing the chance that an existing precedent will be overruled by the court that made it.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** A court's belief that its earlier decision was wrong is not enough to overrule; if it were, stare decisis would mean nothing. Something more is required: error of unusual magnitude or seriously harmful consequences. The heightened threshold is what makes horizontal precedent a real but non-absolute constraint. The underlying values are reliance, settlement and decisional economy; the author cites the view that it is often more important for a matter to be settled than settled correctly, while recording strong critics of the doctrine.

When monitoring change, treat academic or judicial criticism of a precedent as a weak signal on its own.

- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Assuming stability is absolute; the threshold can be met.
- **Repair implication:** Change-risk field on key authorities: "criticised", "pending challenge in the court able to overrule", "overruled/limited".

#### SC1-16
- **ID:** SC1-16
- **Location:** ch 4 §4.2, pp. 67-70
- **Problem addressed:** A better classification of sources than "binding vs persuasive".
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** The author argues "persuasive authority" is a confused label: if a source is used because its reasoning convinces, it is not operating as authority at all. He proposes three classes defined by the decision-maker's freedom: mandatory (must be used and followed or distinguished: governing constitution, statutes, decisions of higher courts in the hierarchy), optional (may be used: decisions of other jurisdictions or lower courts, treatises, journal articles, some non-legal materials) and impermissible (may not properly be relied on at all). The line between optional-but-permissible and impermissible matters as much as the line between mandatory and optional.

- **Production responsibility affected:** authority verification, research, drafting, evaluation

Research is incomplete until mandatory sources have been searched; optional sources cannot substitute for them. The "not permissible" class is a hard filter on what may appear in a work product as support.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** Given a mixed source list, the output orders and labels sources by status for the named forum and refuses to rest a conclusion on optional sources where a mandatory source on point exists. Fail if a secondary source is cited for a point governed by an uncited statute or binding case in the record.

#### SC1-17
- **ID:** SC1-17
- **Location:** ch 4 §4.2, pp. 70-72 (incl. n. 26)
- **Problem addressed:** Why and how optional sources are legitimately relied on, and the weakness in doing so.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** Optional sources are used in two distinct ways. Sometimes the user is convinced by the content, in which case the citation is an acknowledgement of where the idea came from. More often the user defers to the source because it is thought more reliable than the user's own judgment on that subject (a specialist court, a leading treatise); that is genuine reliance on authority, chosen rather than imposed. #### SC1-18
- **ID:** SC1-18
- **Location:** ch 4 §4.2, pp. 72-75 (incl. n. 27)
- **Problem addressed:** What a citation offered as "support" actually shows.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** Legal citation convention often demands only that some court or source has previously said the same thing. Such support can coexist with more, or weightier, sources saying the opposite. What it guarantees is minimal unoriginality: the argument was not invented from nothing, and in law a proposition is somewhat stronger for having been said before. The author calls the practice questionable and often close to pointless, but a weak form of genuine authority. Whether the support requirement really constrains depends on an empirical question he leaves open: if some source can be found for almost any proposition (as some Realists claimed) it constrains little; if authorities often lie all on one side, it constrains a lot. He notes judges' experience that some desired conclusions cannot be supported from the available materials. A footnote mentions a professional-conduct obligation to disclose directly contrary controlling authority and says compliance is imperfect.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** US brief- and opinion-writing conventions; the underlying logic is general.
- **Production responsibility affected:** research, authority verification, risk communication, review/redlining, evaluation
- **Workflow / decision implication:** Finding one supporting source must never be reported as "the law is X". Research must actively look for contrary authority and report the balance: mandatory sources each way, then optional sources each way. Confidence language must track that balance, with distinct phrasing for "supported by at least one source", "weight of authority favours", and "settled by mandatory authority". If no support can be found for a desired proposition, report that plainly.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** Record contains one favourable and several contrary decisions: pass requires the output to disclose the contrary decisions and characterise the balance. Fail if only the favourable cite appears. Separate check: no fabricated or stretched citation where support is absent.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Confirmation-biased retrieval (searching for the desired proposition only); an AI system generating a plausible cite to satisfy the support convention.
- **Repair implication:** Mandatory "contrary authority searched: yes/no, results" field; tiered confidence vocabulary tied to the balance.
- **Proposed disposition:** retain — high value for confidence language and adverse-authority duty; the professional-conduct point is a research question.

#### SC1-19
- **ID:** SC1-19
- **Location:** ch 4 §4.4, pp. 77-80 (incl. nn. 35-36); §4.5, pp. 80-84 (incl. n. 48)
- **Problem addressed:** Which sources are legitimate to cite at all, and how that set changes.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** Some sources are off-limits or disfavoured in a given forum: decisions designated non-precedential or non-citable, official summaries that are not part of the judgment, legal encyclopaedias and crowd-sourced references. The rationale is that such material was not prepared with future cases in mind or is not reliable enough to be taken seriously. Citing a source does two things: it vouches for that source and it signals that sources of that type are legitimate; hence the heat in debates over citing foreign law or non-legal material. What counts as a proper source is mostly fixed by evolving professional practice rather than formal rules, and sources migrate over time from impermissible to optional to effectively mandatory. An optional source can become mandatory in practice: omitting the treatise every local court relies on, or the leading specialist court, would be professionally hazardous. The author reports that US courts increasingly cite non-legal and self-located materials and treats this as a change in what counts as law.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** Heavily forum- and era-specific; the specific rules mentioned are as of 2009 and must be verified. The three-stage migration is the author's descriptive model.
- **Production responsibility affected:** authority verification, research, drafting, review/redlining, product consistency
- **Workflow / decision implication:** Maintain, per forum, a source-type policy: citable as authority, citable with restriction, usable only as a research lead, not to be cited. Check every case's precedential/citability designation before use. Identify, per practice area and forum, the de facto mandatory secondary sources and specialist courts and treat failure to consult them as a research defect. Material suitable for finding the law (encyclopaedias, wikis, headnotes, editorial summaries) must not appear as support in a work product.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** Output never cites a headnote, summary, encyclopaedia or wiki as authority; flags any decision whose designation restricts citation; consults the forum's leading treatise where the knowledge base lists one. Fail on any violation.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Static whitelist that ignores evolving practice; assuming US citation liberality elsewhere.
- **Repair implication:** Forum-keyed source-policy table with review date; citability field on case records.
- **Proposed disposition:** adapt — principle retained; all specifics need current, jurisdiction-specific research.

#### SC1-20
- **ID:** SC1-20
- **Location:** ch 5 §5.1, pp. 85-91
- **Problem addressed:** Confusing analogy with precedent.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** An analogy is chosen: the arguer picks, from many possible earlier situations, the one most helpful for deciding or persuading, and nobody selects an analogy that cuts against their preferred course. A precedent is not chosen: where the current question is inescapably the same as one already decided by a court that binds, the earlier decision imposes itself and can block the preferred outcome; it is not open to the decision-maker to escape by picking a different source case. Loose use of "precedent" for any earlier reported decision fosters the confusion. Though any two cases differ in some way, in practice sameness is often obvious and unchallengeable.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** General common-law reasoning; the apex-court examples are illustrative only, and the author says constraint is more visible in intermediate courts.
- **Production responsibility affected:** research, reasoning/applicability, authority verification, review/redlining
- **Workflow / decision implication:** Separate, in research and in outputs, (a) controlling authorities that must be dealt with whether helpful or not, from (b) analogical material selected because it helps. Research for (a) must be exhaustive and indifferent to which side it favours; (b) is advocacy and should be labelled so. Reviewers should be alert that analogies in a draft were selected for their direction and ask what analogies point the other way.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** Output sections distinguish "controlling authority" from "analogous/supporting decisions". Fail if an out-of-hierarchy or factually remote case is presented as controlling, or if research stops at helpful analogies without a controlling-authority search.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Retrieval systems that return "similar" cases encourage treating everything as analogy; conversely calling a merely analogous case a "precedent" overstates its force.
- **Repair implication:** Two-pass research protocol: controlling-authority pass first, analogy pass second, with separate labels.
- **Proposed disposition:** retain.

#### SC1-21
- **ID:** SC1-21
- **Location:** ch 5 §5.2-5.3, pp. 92-100; §5.4, pp. 100-102
- **Problem addressed:** What makes an analogy good, and whether analogy does independent work.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** Since everything resembles everything else in some respect, a good analogy rests on a relevant similarity, and relevance comes from somewhere. Where a controlling earlier decision has already declared which features matter, the "analogy" is really application of that rule and shades into precedent. Where it has not, the choice between duelling analogies turns on which the decision-maker finds more persuasive. Sceptics (the author cites Posner, Alexander and others) argue that in the second situation the court must itself adopt a rule of relevance on grounds of policy or principle, and that talk of analogy disguises that creative choice as compulsion and avoids stating the rule. Defenders reply that people do perceive similarity directly and rationalise afterwards. The author leaves the dispute partly to cognitive science, but concludes that a relevance-determining rule is central to analogy and that it matters whether that rule pre-exists the analogy. He adds that, logically, an analogy can stretch any distance; analogies persuade only when the step feels small, which is a fact about thinking rather than logic.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** General; the debate is unresolved by the author's own account.
- **Production responsibility affected:** reasoning/applicability, research, drafting, review/redlining, risk communication
- **Workflow / decision implication:** Every analogy in a work product must state: the feature relied on, the principle that makes that feature legally relevant, the source of that principle (announced by controlling authority / found in other authority / supplied by us), and the main relevant differences the other side will invoke. If the principle is supplied by the drafter, the conclusion is an argument, not a statement of law, and must be rated accordingly. Prefer short analogical steps.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** For an "is X more like A or like B" task, pass requires an explicit relevance principle with its source and at least one counter-analogy or relevant difference. Fail if the output asserts similarity by listing shared features only.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Surface-feature matching (a known weakness of similarity search and of language models); hiding a policy choice behind "this is analogous to".
- **Repair implication:** Analogy template with the four fields above; reviewer check for unstated relevance principles.
- **Proposed disposition:** retain — includes the author's sceptical material as contrary evidence to naive "reasoning by analogy".

#### SC1-22
- **ID:** SC1-22
- **Location:** ch 6 §6.1, pp. 104-106; §6.3, pp. 112-117; §6.4, pp. 117-119; §6.5, pp. 121-123
- **Problem addressed:** How judge-made rules change, and what that means for the reliability of a "settled" common-law rule.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** Common-law rules are extracted from decisions, may never have a canonical wording, and are defeasible: a court can modify a rule in the act of applying it, typically by adding an exception when an unforeseen situation or changed social and economic conditions make the existing rule produce a bad result. Such change is retroactive; it is applied to the very parties before the court, who may have planned on the old rule. The author records Dworkin's contrary view that such cases involve no change because underlying principles were always part of the law, and notes it is contested. He then poses Bentham's objection: if any suboptimal result justifies changing the rule, the rules do no work. His answer is that common-law rules function as rules only if they have weight enough to survive some suboptimal results, being modified for seriously bad results but not mildly bad ones; how much weight they actually have is left open. He also observes that doctrines harden with age and that equity, once a free corrective, has itself become rule-bound.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** Common-law jurisdictions and judge-made doctrine; less applicable to detailed statutory regimes, which the author says now dominate. The optimism that change tends toward improvement is described as a traditional belief, with a footnoted literature on biases in common-law evolution (ch 6 n. 10).
- **Production responsibility affected:** risk communication, change impact, reasoning/applicability, drafting
- **Workflow / decision implication:** When advice or a drafted position rests on a judge-made rule, note whether the present facts are of a kind the rule was formed for. If the facts are novel (new technology, new market structure) and the rule yields a markedly harsh result, flag a modification risk and that any modification would apply to the client's existing arrangements. Do not flag such risk merely because the result is somewhat unattractive. Where there is no canonical formulation, say the rule statement is a synthesis and cite the cases it is drawn from.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** Scenario with an old judge-made rule applied to novel facts and a severe result: pass requires a retroactive-modification warning. Scenario with a mildly inconvenient result on ordinary facts: pass requires no such alarm. Rule statements for common-law doctrines cite the source cases.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Quoting a synthesized common-law rule as if it were statutory text; treating every common-law rule as perpetually unstable.
- **Repair implication:** "Source of rule: legislation / judge-made (synthesised)" field; novelty-plus-severity trigger for change-risk notes.
- **Proposed disposition:** adapt.

#### SC1-23
- **ID:** SC1-23
- **Location:** ch 6 §6.2, pp. 110-112; ch 4 §4.4 n. 35, p. 78; ch 1, pp. 8-9
- **Problem addressed:** The risk of generalising a rule from one vivid case.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** Making rules in the course of resolving a single concrete dispute assumes that dispute is representative of its class. Psychological research on availability suggests people over-generalise from whatever is vivid and in front of them, so a decision-maker immersed in one case may set a rule ill-suited to the rest of the class. The traditional reply is that later cases allow correction over time, approached by successive over- and under-shooting. Relatedly, courts sometimes limit the future use of a decision to avoid a reason crafted for one case misfiring in others, and law-school and appellate questioning by hypotheticals exists to test a proposed rule against other cases in the class.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** The author presents the availability concern as a possibility, not a demonstrated defect. By extension (my inference, not the author's) it applies to building templates, playbooks or clause standards from one matter.
- **Production responsibility affected:** drafting, product consistency, evaluation, change impact
- **Workflow / decision implication:** Before turning a one-matter solution into a reusable clause, playbook position or skill rule, test it against a set of varied hypotheticals from the same class and record the scenarios where it misfires. Treat first versions as provisional and schedule revision after further matters.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** A proposed standard position is accompanied by a hypothetical test set with recorded results. Fail if a rule derived from a single incident is promoted to standard without such testing.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Over-engineering every clause; treating the extension beyond adjudication as the author's claim.
- **Repair implication:** Hypothetical-battery step in template/playbook governance.
- **Proposed disposition:** adapt — author's point is about judicial lawmaking; extension to legal-product governance is ours.

### 2. Material findings

#### SC2-01
- **ID:** SC2-01
- **Location:** ch 7, §7.1 "Do Rules and Precedents Decide Cases?", pp. 126-127 (print); ch 7, §7.4 "Realism and the Role of the Lawyer", pp. 142-143 (print)
- **Problem addressed:** What a person asking for legal advice actually wants to know.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** From the adviser's standpoint, the useful product is a forecast of how the legal system will in fact react to a course of conduct, not only a restatement of what the written rule says. Ordinary clients (not "bad" ones: a publisher, a taxpayer, a driver, a testator) rationally want to know where enforcement and adjudication in practice diverge from the text. The author endorses this predictive framing while noting it is not the only legitimate viewpoint on law.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** Advisory (ex ante) work. US-centred but the point is general. It presupposes that information about actual official behaviour is available; the book gives no method for obtaining it beyond doctrine, experienced-lawyer perception and empirical study.
- **Production responsibility affected:** scoping, reasoning/applicability, risk communication
- **Workflow / decision implication:** An advice output should separate (a) what the governing text/doctrine says, (b) the expected behaviour of the relevant decision-maker or enforcer, and (c) the evidential basis for (b). Where the agent has no evidence for (b) beyond doctrine, it must say so rather than present the doctrinal answer as a forecast.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** In an advice memo, is there an explicit statement of what the prediction is a prediction of (which body, which stage), and is the basis for any claimed gap between text and practice identified? Pass/fail.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Treating "what officials tolerate" as permission (the speeding-tolerance example is descriptive, not a compliance standard); an AI system inventing enforcement-practice claims without a source; ignoring professional-conduct limits on advising non-compliance.
- **Repair implication:** Where a draft states only doctrine, add a "practical outcome" limb or an explicit statement that no practice evidence was available; where a draft asserts practice, require a source or downgrade to assumption.
- **Proposed disposition:** adapt — sound framing for advice structure, but must be fenced by compliance and ethics rules the book does not address.

#### SC2-02
- **ID:** SC2-02
- **Location:** ch 7, §7.3 "An Empirical Claim", pp. 139-142 (print); ch 7, §7.5, p. 147 (print)
- **Problem addressed:** How much weight doctrine deserves as a predictor of outcomes, and where it is weakest.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** Whether formal legal materials drive outcomes is an empirical question whose answer varies by domain. The author reports that research supports the realist picture most strongly for an apex court with a self-selected docket and ideologically loaded issues, and much less for lower courts, where legal factors explain considerably more (though non-legal factors still matter more than the traditional model admits). He offers as a plausible but untested hypothesis that non-doctrinal explanations are stronger for (i) ideologically charged issues, (ii) higher appellate courts than trial courts, (iii) messy common law than statutory interpretation. He adds that for statutory questions ordinary meaning, and for appellate contract questions conventional contract doctrine, may well be the main determinant.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** US courts; empirical literature as of 2009; the three-factor gradient is the author's hypothesis, explicitly not established by the research he cites. Judges' own reports of being law-bound are said probably to overstate doctrine's effect.
- **Production responsibility affected:** risk communication, reasoning/applicability, escalation, evaluation
- **Workflow / decision implication:** Confidence language should be conditioned on forum level, how value-laden the issue is, and whether the source is statute or case law. High-level, value-laden, common-law questions warrant wider uncertainty bands and earlier specialist escalation; technical statutory questions in lower forums warrant more reliance on text and doctrine.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** Does the uncertainty statement name at least one of these drivers (forum, issue type, source type) as a reason for the stated confidence level, rather than giving an unexplained hedge? Pass/fail.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Turning the hypothesis into a numeric formula; importing US apex-court findings to other jurisdictions or to transactional questions; using "realism" as an excuse for blanket hedging.
- **Repair implication:** Replace generic hedges with reasoned, factor-based confidence statements; where the factors point to low doctrinal determinacy, route to a human specialist with forum knowledge.
- **Proposed disposition:** qualify — useful calibration heuristic, but author marks it as hypothesis and US-specific; needs corroboration from other corpus books or research.

#### SC2-03
- **ID:** SC2-03
- **Location:** ch 7, §7.2 "Does Doctrine Constrain Even If It Does Not Direct?", pp. 137-138 (print); ch 8, §8.2, p. 157 (print); ch 9, §9.4, pp. 184-185 (print)
- **Problem addressed:** Inferring how determinate the law is from the cases one reads.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** Reported appellate decisions are a skewed sample: matters where the law clearly favours one side are seldom disputed, litigated or appealed, so what reaches the reports is concentrated at the law's fuzzy edges. Most everyday applications of law are easy. The realist indeterminacy claim is therefore best read as a claim about hard appellate cases, not about law generally. The author notes an exception: where a litigant bears no cost and gains side-benefits from appealing (his example is incarcerated appellants), weak cases do reach appellate courts in volume.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** Depends on parties bearing litigation costs and acting on rational expectations; exception where incentives differ. General to common-law systems.
- **Production responsibility affected:** research, reasoning/applicability, risk communication, evaluation
- **Workflow / decision implication:** (1) Do not treat the existence of litigated authority on both sides as evidence that a routine question is uncertain. (2) Conversely, when a matter is already in serious dispute between resourced parties, assume each side has a colourable argument and look for it. (3) Do not over-hedge easy cases; say plainly when text plus sensible result settles the point.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** On a benchmark of plainly easy questions (clear text, sensible result), does the agent give a direct answer without manufactured uncertainty? On a benchmark of genuinely contested questions, does it surface the opposing colourable argument?
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Labelling a case "easy" without checking for a bad-answer problem (SC2-12) or open-texture problem (SC2-10); assuming selection logic in settings where cost incentives are absent.
- **Repair implication:** Add an explicit easy/hard classification step with reasons before choosing the depth of analysis and the hedging register.
- **Proposed disposition:** retain — directly actionable calibration principle against both over- and under-confidence.

#### SC2-04
- **ID:** SC2-04
- **Location:** ch 7, §7.2, pp. 135-138 (print); ch 7, §7.3, pp. 138-139 (print)
- **Problem addressed:** Testing how far doctrine actually constrains an outcome.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** The realist claim has two parts: decision-makers often sense a preferred outcome before consulting the law, and in a complex common-law system they can usually (not always) find respectable legal materials to justify it. Doctrine constrains to the extent that no plausible legal justification can be written for the competing outcome; the range of writable justifications is limited, and some preferred results simply "won't write".
- **Assumptions and applicability:** Hard, litigated cases in complex common-law systems. Author treats both parts as empirical and variable.
- **Production responsibility affected:** reasoning/applicability, risk communication, review/redlining, evaluation
- **Workflow / decision implication:** Use a "will the opposite opinion write?" test: before stating a conclusion with confidence, attempt to construct the best professionally respectable justification for the contrary outcome from accepted sources. If one can be built, report the question as open and identify what is likely to tip it; if none can, confidence is warranted.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** For each contested conclusion, does the work product contain a steel-manned contrary argument with its sources, or a recorded finding that none could be constructed? Pass/fail.
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** An AI's fluency makes almost anything "write"; the test requires that the contrary argument rest on real, verified authority and meet professional respectability, not mere rhetorical plausibility.
- **Repair implication:** Couple the test to authority verification; discard contrary arguments that rely on unverified or mischaracterised sources.
- **Proposed disposition:** adapt — strong self-check, but must be bound to verified sources to avoid manufactured indeterminacy.

#### SC2-08
- **ID:** SC2-08
- **Location:** ch 8, §8.2, pp. 156-158 (print)
- **Problem addressed:** Choosing the right interpretive method for the problem at hand.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** Sort every interpretive question into one of three types: (1) the words give a plausible answer; (2) the words give no determinate answer; (3) the words give a determinate answer that looks wrong measured against purpose, probable intent or good sense. In type 1 the wording normally ends the matter even if other sensible answers exist, and such questions are rarely even disputed. Types 2 and 3 are the two distinct species of hard case and call for different handling (SC2-10, SC2-12).

- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** Is each interpretive issue tagged with one of the three types, with a reason, and is the downstream treatment consistent with the tag?
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Mis-tagging type 3 as type 2 (pretending clear words are unclear to escape them) or type 1 without checking the result is sensible.

#### SC2-10
- **ID:** SC2-10
- **Location:** ch 8, §8.3 "When the Text Provides No Answer", pp. 158-163 and nn. 25, 30, 31 (print)
- **Problem addressed:** What to do when the words run out.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** Two different sources of indeterminacy: (a) pervasively vague wording ("reasonable", "best interests", "hazardous") that needs outside supplementation in nearly every contested application; (b) normally precise wording that becomes uncertain only when an unforeseen application arises (open texture, which the author distinguishes from vagueness as the standing possibility of vagueness). In both, no amount of staring at the words helps; the interpreter must go somewhere else, and where to go is contested: policy judgment as if legislating (Hart), statutory purpose (Fuller), recorded legislative intent, coherence with the rest of the legal system (Dworkin), efficiency, equities. Purpose is a property of the text and can often be inferred with confidence from the four corners (what else the provision lists or regulates) or from an express purpose statement; intention is a property of legislators and is evidenced by extrinsic history, whose use is disputed and varies by jurisdiction (the author reports British practice as far less receptive than US practice). Even a very vague statute still constrains by its evident goal.
- **Assumptions and applicability:** US debate as of 2009; jurisdiction-specific rules on extrinsic materials must be researched, not assumed.
- **Production responsibility affected:** research, reasoning/applicability, risk communication, escalation
- **Workflow / decision implication:** In a no-answer case: (1) say which kind of indeterminacy it is; (2) first try to infer purpose from the instrument itself (companion items in lists, stated objects); (3) only then consult extrinsic material, after checking whether the forum admits it; (4) present the outcome as an exercise of discretion by the decision-maker, with lower confidence, and name the supplementation source relied on.
- **Evaluation evidence or criterion:** Does the analysis name the supplementation source used and justify its admissibility in the relevant forum? Does it avoid presenting a purposive conclusion as if dictated by the words?
- **Failure / misuse conditions:** Conflating purpose with legislative intent; citing legislative history in a forum that restricts it; asserting a single "purpose" where the text supports several (the vehicles example shows different purposes produce different results).
- **Repair implication:** Where purpose is contestable, run the analysis under each candidate purpose and report divergence.
- **Proposed disposition:** retain — directly shapes an applicability workflow; extrinsic-materials rules flagged for jurisdictional research.

#### SC2-12
- **ID:** SC2-12
- **Location:** ch 8, §8.4 "When the Text Provides a Bad Answer", pp. 163-167 and n. 34 (print); ch 12, §12.2 "Presumptions", pp. 228-229 (print)
- **Problem addressed:** Clear words that produce an absurd or purpose-defeating result.
- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** The literal meaning operates as a rebuttable presumption: it is the default, and the party arguing against it carries a heavy burden ("swimming upstream"), but such arguments are possible and in the US often succeed. Even committed textualists accept an exception for obvious absurdity or evident drafting slip. Beyond absurdity, where judges are trusted, literal meaning may yield to common sense, probable intent or purpose. The counter-position is about who decides: absurdity is partly in the eye of the beholder, the legislature voted only on words, and empowering anyone to override text has rule-of-law costs. In ch 12 the author generalises: US law can be described as presuming the formal rule or precedent controls unless the burdened party shows the result is not merely wrong but very wrong. #### SC2-26
- **ID:** SC2-26

- **Principle / method / heuristic (own words):** Legal questions are rarely only "what is right?" but also "who is authorised to decide this?". ### 4. Material the author marks as contested, or where the author criticises other approaches

- **Realism's truth is domain-specific, neither largely false nor largely true** (ch 7, §7.5, p. 147). The author rejects "we are all Realists now" as false and calls surviving realism domesticated (p. 144). He offers four competing explanations for realism's limited uptake without choosing among them (pp. 145-146).
- **Caricature of realism rejected:** the "breakfast" quip is treated as unfair to Frank; Frank's psychoanalytic emphasis is called crude and silly, but his core insight (outcome precedes justification) is called sound and widely shared by judges (ch 7, §7.1, pp. 129-131). Extra-legal determinants are compatible with predictability (n. 18).
- **Facts-versus-rules characterisation of realism** is called a false dichotomy; the real dispute is over which rules/norms select the relevant facts (ch 7, §7.1, p. 132).
- **Holmes** is treated as a forebear, not a realist, because he thought doctrinal categories were the best predictors (pp. 126-127).
- **Llewellyn's duelling-canons thesis:** the author largely accepts it (ch 8, §8.5, p. 170) but records that its accuracy is disputed (ch 7, §7.2, p. 137 n. 29) and that Llewellyn limited the claim to cases worth fighting over and later recanted the "pretty playthings" remark (p. 131 n. 21).
- **Judges' self-reports** probably overstate the role of formal law (ch 7, §7.3, pp. 140-141).
- **Bentham's prediction** that detailed codes would make interpretation rare is called plainly wrong (ch 8, §8.1, p. 150).
- **Fuller's claim that words have no context-independent meaning** is called a philosophical mistake that undercuts his otherwise valuable point about purpose-defeating literal results (ch 8, §8.2, pp. 155-156).
- **Dworkin's "real rule behind the text"** is called more mysterious than helpful; whether judges should seek system-wide fit even when text is clear is flagged as ongoing controversy (ch 8, §8.3, p. 158 n. 23, p. 161 n. 29).
- **Textualism versus intentionalism/purposivism:** both sides set out; the author describes strict literalism even in absurd cases as plausible but not prevailing (ch 8, §§8.3-8.4, pp. 159-166). "Ambiguity" is said to be the wrong word for the usual problem (p. 162 n. 30).
- **Judicial candour:** the author argues its importance is substantially exaggerated, against the intuitive pro-candour view (ch 9, §9.1, pp. 173-175).
- **Holding/dicta:** traditional necessity test criticised as theoretically and practically fragile; sceptical reading offered but then limited (ch 9, §9.3, pp. 181-184).
- **Narrow versus broad judicial reasons (minimalism):** author notes the minimalist position and his own contrary view favouring guidance (ch 9, §9.2, p. 180 n. 20; ch 10, §10.4, pp. 197-199). He calls the objection that courts act illegitimately when they issue rule-like directives "odd".
- **Non-precedential opinions:** controversial; the author finds little fault with them (ch 9, §9.4, pp. 186-187).
- **Rules versus standards:** repeatedly said to have no right answer in general; neither is more faithful to the rule of law (ch 10, §§10.2-10.4, pp. 194, 195, 199). Author also laments the lack of serious literature on rule-making technique (p. 202 n. 25).
- **Law/fact distinction:** a contrary view that it is a myth is cited; the author defends it as is/ought while conceding normative elements leak into descriptions (ch 11, §11.1, pp. 204-205 and n. 8).
- **Adversarial fact-finding and exclusionary evidence rules:** presented as peculiar, not self-evidently reliable, criticised by Bentham and modern epistemologists, and in decline (ch 11, §11.2, pp. 207-211 and n. 14).
- **Appellate courts' unsupported empirical premises:** the author criticises reliance on intuition and "armchair sociology" and treats how appellate courts should obtain general facts as unresolved (ch 11, §11.3, pp. 214-218).
- **Irrebuttable presumptions:** the view that they are unfair is rejected as misunderstanding how rules work (ch 12, §12.2, pp. 226-227).
- **Law knowingly accepts sub-optimal outcomes** for institutional reasons; the author notes some regard this as a defect (ch 11, §11.2, p. 212; ch 12, §12.3, p. 233).

### 6. Candidate benchmark cases suggested by the text (own words, synthetic)

1. **Easy-case discipline.** A client asks whether a filing due "on or before 30 June" can be lodged on 2 July without consequence. Clear text, sensible result. Pass: direct answer, no manufactured uncertainty, brief note of any relief mechanism to be checked. Fail: multi-page hedging or purposive speculation. (SC2-03, SC2-08)
2. **Three-way triage.** A site rule bans "motorised equipment" in a warehouse aisle. Three scenarios: a forklift (plausible answer); a worker's powered mobility aid (bad answer candidate); a hand pallet-jack with a small electric assist (no clear answer). Pass: each scenario tagged correctly, literal result stated first in the second scenario, burden placed on the party escaping the text, supplementation source named in the third. (SC2-08, SC2-10, SC2-12)
3. **Purpose from the four corners.** Two versions of a lease clause: one bans "vehicles, amplified music and generators" from a courtyard; the other bans "vehicles and open fires". Ask whether an electric cargo bike is caught. Pass: agent infers different purposes from the companion items, reaches different or differently hedged answers, and says the inference is from the text itself. (SC2-10)
4. **Canon-only trap.** A supply contract lists "strikes, floods, fires and other events" as relief events; client wants to claim for a supplier's insolvency. Pass: agent identifies the genus-limiting reading as a risk, presents the opposing reading, does not rest a confident conclusion on either canon, and proposes redrafting for future contracts. Fail: "ejusdem generis therefore excluded/included" as a one-line answer. (SC2-13)
5. **Will the opposite write?** Give the agent a contested common-law question with verified authorities on both sides and ask for a confident answer. Pass: agent constructs the contrary justification from the real authorities, reports the question as open, and identifies the likely tipping factors. Second variant with authorities all one way: pass if the agent reports that no respectable contrary argument could be built. (SC2-04)
6. **Calibration by forum and issue type.** Same doctrinal question posed twice: once as a technical statutory point before a first-instance tribunal, once as a value-laden point headed to an apex court. Pass: confidence language differs and the difference is explained by forum/issue/source factors; escalation recommended in the second. (SC2-02)
7. **Register split.** Ask for (a) a client advice note and (b) a skeleton argument on the same point. Pass: (a) has likelihood language and downside; (b) has reasons and authority with no success-probability talk addressed to the tribunal. (SC2-06)
8. **Adverse dictum.** Research bundle includes a binding court's broad statement against the client, made in a case with distant facts. Pass: agent does not omit it or wave it away as dicta; records status, generality and factual distance; acknowledges the explanatory burden it creates. (SC2-15)
9. **Status check.** Bundle includes a decision marked as non-precedential and a note that the top court declined review of a favourable lower decision. Pass: agent flags the first as limited/non-citable pending rule check and refuses to describe the second as endorsement. (SC2-16)
10. **Precision choice in drafting.** Client (a customer) asks for a service-credit clause. Options: "response within a reasonable time" versus "response within 4 business hours". Pass: agent identifies who holds discretion under each, links the choice to who will operate the clause and the client's need to plan, and notes misfit risk of the bright line. (SC2-17)
11. **Breadth versus vagueness.** Review an indemnity covering "all losses arising from any breach" (broad, precise) and another covering "serious reputational harm" (narrow, vague). Pass: comments diagnose the two axes separately with matched fixes. (SC2-19)
12. **Obsolescence.** A ten-year data agreement defines permitted transfer methods by a closed list of named technologies. Pass: agent flags obsolescence, proposes a functional definition with non-exhaustive examples, and records the certainty trade-off. (SC2-18)
13. **Single-matter overfitting.** Agent is asked to turn a clause negotiated in one unusual deal into the firm's standard. Pass: agent asks for or generates varied scenarios, tests the clause, and limits the template's stated scope. (SC2-20)
14. **Fact/characterisation unpacking.** Instruction email says "the supplier repudiated the contract and we terminated with reasonable notice". Pass: agent decomposes into raw facts needed (what was said/done, dates, notice text), tags each by provenance, and makes conclusions conditional. (SC2-21, SC2-22)
15. **Hidden empirical premise.** Draft advice states a clause is acceptable because "this is market standard and regulators never enforce this". Pass on review: both statements flagged as unsourced empirical premises, with request for support or re-labelling as assumption. (SC2-23)
16. **Burden table.** A four-element claim where three elements are strong and one rests on a contested recollection; the defence raises an affirmative defence on which the defendant must first produce evidence. Pass: element/burden/standard/evidence table; overall assessment driven by the weak element; default outcome stated if the contested fact cannot be proved. (SC2-24)
17. **Deeming clause.** Notice clause says notices are "deemed received two days after posting" versus "presumed received unless the recipient shows otherwise". Pass: agent classifies the first as conclusive (substantive rule that can misfire) and the second as rebuttable, and advises according to which side the client is on. (SC2-25)
18. **Standard of review.** Client lost before a specialist regulator on a fact-heavy discretionary decision and asks about appeal prospects because "the decision was wrong". Pass: agent identifies the standard of review first, explains that mere error may not suffice, separates any pure legal-interpretation ground that attracts fresh review, and flags the doctrine for current-law verification. (SC2-26)
